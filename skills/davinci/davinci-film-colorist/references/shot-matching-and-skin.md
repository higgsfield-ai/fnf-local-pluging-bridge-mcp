# Skin and Shot Matching

Match the relationships that persist within a scene: relevant surfaces, lighting, tonal character, palette, and texture. Use a stable reference shot and compare the last corrected shot back to it to detect accumulated drift. Framing, object area, and background can alter global measurements without changing the subject's appropriate exposure.

Compare corresponding materials and illuminated and shadowed sides before adjusting the whole image. A close view can feel brighter because the face occupies more area; a perceptual correction may help, but matching the average of the whole frame is not the objective. If isolated skin matches while the full frames differ, inspect the surrounding colors and light.

Diagnose black point, midtones, saturation, hue, and texture separately. A faded impression need not be a white-balance error. A correct hue with low chroma may need saturation. If a global correction improves skin while damaging a neutral object, reconsider its tonal range or local scope. When color and contrast match but the cut still feels discontinuous, inspect noise, sharpness, grain, and motion.

Skin is part of the lighting and can legitimately carry source color. Redness can arise from material, makeup, expression, or illumination. Do not force a single abstract healthy hue. Preserve individual brightness, hue, and saturation relationships among people while making important subjects readable according to the brief.

An explicit request to lighten a chosen person's skin permits that targeted creative correction; check consistency across face, neck, and hands, texture, and plausible light. It does not imply assigning every person the same scope level. Skin Tone Indicator is not a universal target.

For noisy material, compare a smaller exposure demand or a less demanding common look before accepting destructive smoothing. Adding texture to a cleaner insert is an optional artistic choice. Check the result in motion at a comparable viewing scale; downstream encoding remains a separate verification.

Stop when the scene no longer reveals unintended jumps, meaningful objects agree, and additional local correction offers no useful improvement. Apply stricter matching when the brief requires exact background, costume, or product color, and retain explicit user feedback as an acceptance condition.
