# Visual Intention and Evaluation

## Attention before correction

Identify the main action or subject, important material or product properties, and the surroundings that support them. Evaluate brightness, contrast boundaries, hue, saturation, area, depth, and motion together. Nearness to the camera does not establish narrative priority. A face need not be the brightest object to remain important.

Name a specific competing region before proposing a local correction. Consider whether reducing its brightness or saturation would be more useful than lifting the subject. Preserve the direction and softness of existing illumination and the information carried by reflections, folds, and shadows. Do not turn the background into an evenly dark layer.

## Form the visual intention

Extract the requested mood, contrast, palette, material treatment, texture, and unwanted effects from the current brief and available references. Mark inferred preferences as hypotheses. A user's culture, language, nationality, or a subject's appearance does not supply an unspoken palette preference. A named creative reference is runtime material to analyze, not a fixed style preset.

Describe the desired relationships with observable terms. Choose which regions provide dark and light anchors, which hues are allowed to compete, whether skin retains colored illumination, and which texture matters. A complementary palette is optional. Keep the chosen baseline visible during exploration.

## Evaluate the whole image

Judge a change by its role in the composition and scene as well as its local effect. A technically neutral patch can be artistically wrong under colored light. Distinguish a display error, exposure problem, white-balance problem, and deliberate lighting before correcting.

Compare an appropriately expressive candidate with an attenuated version when the brief supports it. A strong slider value is not intrinsically excessive, and a small value is not evidence of good judgment. Stop when the intended relationship is established without losing material distinctions, spatial depth, useful skin variation, or plausible light.

Record concise observable reasons for accepting, weakening, or undoing a change. Do not present an aesthetic preference as an objective measurement or reproduce a detailed internal monologue. Preserve meaningful user feedback and distinguish it from a lack of response.
