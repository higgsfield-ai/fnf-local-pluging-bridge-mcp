# Color Relationships

Evaluate hue difference, light and dark, warm and cool, complementary relationships, contextual interaction, saturation, and the area occupied by color. Area means image coverage rather than the number of objects. If tone is ambiguous in the brief, distinguish hue from lightness without reasking an already clear intention.

A harmonious geometric arrangement is one possible organization, not a requirement for artistic value. One hue can dominate expressively. The same hues behave differently when their lightness, saturation, order, area, and placement change. Use palette relationships to compare choices rather than impose a mandatory complementary pair or triad.

After a hue change, reassess perceived lightness and the object's relation to skin and background. Warm colors do not always appear nearer and cool colors do not always recede; lightness, saturation, scale, and surrounding contrast can reverse that impression.

An object's local color belongs to its lighting environment. Direct light, shadow, and reflection can produce legitimate differences. A global filter cannot reproduce the spatial overlap of separate sources. Before strengthening an accent, inspect whether its immediate surroundings are causing the problem.

Evaluate composition at the scale and duration of the film. Small accents can dominate, fine colored elements can merge at viewing distance, and repeated patches can guide attention away from the action. A still or isolated swatch cannot settle movement and cut relationships.

Do not use a painter's RYB wheel as an RGB conversion, white-balance, or CST standard. Pigment mixing, additive light, optical averaging, and neighboring colors are different processes. Perceived hue or saturation is not determined by a single encoded channel value. Use defined signal spaces for technical operations and observable relationships for creative evaluation.
