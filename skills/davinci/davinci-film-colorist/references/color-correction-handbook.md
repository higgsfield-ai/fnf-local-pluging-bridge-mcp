# Correction Principles

## Diagnose before choosing an operation

Establish the source, actual viewing path, and intended scene before deciding that an image is wrong. A display interpretation problem can resemble an exposure, balance, or saturation defect. Check a plausible cause against the native image and relevant scopes instead of compensating for unknown transforms with grading controls.

Compare important surfaces and light relationships rather than global averages. A neutral reference must be credible under the actual light; a colored reflection or clipped channel is not a reliable white-balance sample. Separate hue error, weak chroma, black-point placement, and midtone readability so one problem does not accumulate unrelated corrections.

## Tonal relationships

When lowering blacks destroys hair or dark fabric, a stronger light anchor can increase perceived shadow depth without further black clipping. Inspect highlight detail and the scene's overall character before accepting that approach. It does not guarantee numerically unchanged shadows or impose a universal output range.

A desired highlight hue can require a restrained opposing adjustment in an overlapping lower range. Judge the combined result and preserved highlights; this does not narrow the original control's influence. If the compensation damages another region, the intended separation has not been achieved. Use actual control behavior rather than a conflicting arrow or outdated diagram in historical material.

## Targeted correction

When a difficult boundary determines selection quality, sample the intended object near that boundary without crossing into the background. For an ordinary initial HSL selection, prefer representative values between its bright and dark regions. Try a better sample before complicating a key built from one noisy pixel.

A signal-selected range can include different objects. Inspect actual matte coverage, softness, edge artifacts, and changing light. Preserve transparent detail, reflections, and motion blur instead of maximizing binary isolation. Choose local corrections only when they improve the full composition.

## Scene and quality limits

Match meaningful objects while preserving lighting differences, individual skin properties, and scene intent. An isolated patch match can fail in its surroundings. Texture and motion can reveal discontinuity after color already agrees. Keep a stable reference to avoid cumulative drift.

Separate recording losses from damage introduced by the grade. Look for clipped transitions, banding, compression break-up, chroma noise, unstable keys, and temporal artifacts at the stage where they arise. A later darker patch, glow, or sharper edge does not restore lost data.

Historical source readings support these principles but do not prove the current operation. Preserve the distinction between documented mechanics, an adapted decision, an observed control response, and accepted grading quality. Consult the retained source evidence only when provenance or a specific recorded method matters.
