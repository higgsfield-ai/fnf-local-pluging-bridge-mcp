# Scopes During Grading

Begin with an image observation and locate the relevant subject's contribution on the scope. A red trace might come from a wall rather than skin. Confirm spatial correspondence before interpreting channel balance or changing a global control.

When improving midtone readability, compare the target surface with important shadows and highlights, then inspect texture after the native tonal adjustment. A waveform shift establishes a signal response, not recovered scene exposure in stops. For contrast, follow separation between meaningful dark and light anchors rather than maximizing trace width.

For an unwanted common cast, use a credible neutral under known light and compare its channels. A global balance adjustment should not erase motivated differences among sources. If hue is already correct but colors are weak, inspect saturation before continuing to move white balance.

For global desaturation, a vectorscope contraction should correspond to the intended change in the RGB image. It does not establish unchanged luminance, correct source white balance, or gamut compliance. For a selected hue shift or chroma reduction, inspect other objects sharing the range, channel limits, edges, and motion.

When an effect is hard to see, compare only that correction on, off, and on at the same timecode while retaining the output transform and scope settings. Verify trace freshness, parameters, enabled state, and real graph connections before concluding that the control has no effect. Do not increase the correction merely to make the trace move.

A transform's bypass needs separate interpretation because later nodes and color management can still act on its input. Localize a suspected clipping stage by comparing actual RGB channel behavior across the chain. Making a bright plateau darker without returning texture does not establish recovery.

Keep the scope observation, native-frame observation, and quantitative measurement distinct. A qualitative A/B with incompletely known display settings can support a limited observation without proving exact chroma, physical luminance, or calibrated output.
